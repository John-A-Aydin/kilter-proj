UNDER THE HOOD

How the model sets a climb

A small transformer reads the holds you picked, plus a grade and an angle, and predicts the rest of the climb one hold at a time. This page covers how a climb becomes tokens, what the network looks like, how it was trained, and the experiments behind each choice.

01

A climb becomes a sequence

Each hold in a climb has a role: start hand finish foot. A token is one hold in one role, so the vocabulary is 476 holds × 4 roles, plus PAD BOS EOS SEP: 1,908 tokens. The holds you give the model go before SEP. The model predicts everything after it, in a fixed order: starts, then hands, finishes, and feet, each from bottom to top and then left to right.

What gets given during training

Every epoch, each training climb gets a new given set drawn from a mixture. One model can then generate from nothing, from start holds plus some feet, or from any set of picks.

02

Architecture

The model is a causal decoder-only transformer. Two design choices matter most. Hold embeddings know where a hold is on the wall. The output layer reuses the same embedding table, so a hold’s position on the wall shapes how it is read and how it is predicted.

Geometry in the embedding

The baseline learned 1,904 unrelated hold-and-role vectors. Here, nearby holds start out similar, and holds that are rarely used still get a sensible vector.

Tied output weights

The scores for the next hold use the same table that embeds holds, plus a per-token bias. Predicting a hold and reading it share one representation.

Conditions everywhere

The grade and angle pass through sinusoidal features and an MLP, then get added to every position, so their effect can be nonlinear. The baseline used a single linear layer.

04

Decisions and the experiments behind them

Each change started from the same model, the large model trained 8 epochs with half its grades nulled. Every change was trained on three seeds and chosen on validation. Test numbers were checked afterwards.

Other choices

  • Mixture of given sets. 30 / 45 / 25 covers the three ways people use the page. The starts mode always gives every start hold, so given starts mean “these are all the starts.”
  • Leakage removal. 1,033 hold sets appear under several UUIDs. Training copies of held-out hold sets are dropped.
  • Selection metric. The mean nats per climb across scratch, starts, and starts + every other foot. It can be compared with the baseline, unlike per-token loss.
  • No ascent weighting. 42% of climbs have a single ascent, but weighting by log ascents made every mode worse.
  • Deterministic training. torch.use_deterministic_algorithms, with a fixed split seed of 42 for all runs.
05

Decoding rules

At each step, a mask removes only continuations that no training climb has:

  • breaking the canonical order, or repeating a hold
  • more than 2 starts or 2 finishes
  • a foot before a finish has been placed
  • ending before there is at least one start and one finish
  • more than 40 holds

The served model was also trained with this mask (train.rule_loss), so its loss only ranks legal tokens. It relies on the mask: with the rules off, its samples are meaningless. The no-rules row below comes from the earlier large model, trained without the mask.

Caveats

  • Nobody has climbed these. The checks are structural, plus summary statistics such as hold count, feet, finish height, and the largest gap between hand holds.
  • The rules guarantee validity. With rules on, validity mostly measures the mask. The no-rules number for a model trained without the mask is the fairer measure of what a model learned.
  • Grade fidelity is weak. About 50 samples per grade, and the V2 → V11 trend in hold count is noisy. The gain in likelihood is solid; matching the requested grade is not established.
  • One start is out of distribution for the starts mode. In that mode, training always gave every start hold. The random-subset mode partly covers giving just one.
